Methodology

How every number on this site is made, what it stands in for, and where it can mislead.

The core limitation. No public dataset records which park a child played in and whether that child later went pro. This site connects the steps using neighborhood-level stand-ins, so it can show patterns (for example, "neighborhoods with more park courts produce more pros"), not causes.

Geography

The basic unit is the 2020 census block. Each populated block is placed in a neighborhood using its interior point. Neighborhoods for this city come from: . Income and child counts come from census tracts (ACS ). Each tract's counts are split across its blocks in proportion to their 2020 population, then added up by neighborhood. Neighborhoods with fewer than 100 residents (airports, parks, industrial land) are left out.

Step 1 · Park access

Residents within a 10-minute walk of a park

The share of residents whose census block falls inside a Trust for Public Land ParkServe® 10-minute-walk service area. These areas follow the real street and path network (about half a mile), not a straight-line circle.

Park acres per 1,000 residents

Acres of publicly accessible ParkServe parkland inside the neighborhood, per 1,000 residents. National forests, national recreation areas, wildlife refuges and state parks () are excluded. They are huge, and they would make the neighborhoods next to them look park-rich even when kids there have few nearby parks.

Park courts & fields per 1,000 kids

Sports pitches, courts, tracks and sports centers mapped in OpenStreetMap that sit inside a public park, per 1,000 residents under 18. School fields are deliberately excluded: athletes are placed by their high school, so counting school fields would link the two by construction. OpenStreetMap is volunteer-mapped, so coverage may be uneven between neighborhoods.

Step 2 · Youth sports participation (estimated)

The National Survey of Children's Health asks parents whether their child (ages 6–17) played on a sports team or took sports lessons in the past year. Using the public microdata and survey weights (), we calculate the participation rate for each family-income bracket in this state:

Each neighborhood's estimate applies those rates to its own mix of kids by income bracket (ACS table B17024). This estimate is modeled from income, so it rises with income by design. It shows how many kids we'd expect to play, not a local count. Its relationship to income is not reported as a finding.

Step 3 · Going pro

Pro athletes come from Wikidata (public domain). An athlete counts if they played for a team in the NBA, WNBA, NFL, MLB, MLS, NWSL or NHL and Wikidata lists a high school for them inside this area. They are placed at that high school's location.

  • High school is a stand-in for home. Public high schools mostly draw from nearby neighborhoods. Private schools, especially sports powerhouses, recruit from much farther away. That's why there's a "public high schools only" version of every athlete measure.
  • Wikidata is incomplete. Recent, well-known players are better covered than older or short-career players, and not every player's high school is recorded.
  • Eras differ. Athletes born decades ago grew up in neighborhoods that may look very different today. The "born +" measure keeps only recent generations.
  • Small numbers. A single athlete can swing the rate for a small neighborhood. Neighborhoods under residents are faded on the map, and those under are left out of the correlations.
  • Data errors are filtered where we can detect them. For example, schools whose federal (NCES) ID points to another state are dropped, since their map coordinates are wrong.

Statistics

Relationships use Spearman rank correlation across neighborhoods. It's robust to the extreme values common in per-capita rates. Because park access and pro athletes could both just track wealth, we also report partial correlations controlling for median household income: the relationship left after removing what income explains. Income groups split neighborhoods into five bands of roughly equal population.
